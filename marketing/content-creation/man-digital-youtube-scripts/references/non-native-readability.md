# Writing for a Non-Native Speaker to Read Aloud

The presenter is not a native English speaker. Every sentence must be easy to say out loud
on the first take — that's a stricter bar than "reads fine on the page."

## The core rule

One idea per sentence. Target 8–15 words. Hard cap 20. If a sentence needs "and," "which,"
or "because" **plus** a dash, split it into two or three sentences instead.

## Before / after

These are real over-complex sentences a first-draft attempt at this exact task produced,
rewritten to the spoken standard:

❌ Too complex (28 words, three embedded clauses):
"If any one of those four is a mess — no clean discount property, deals with inconsistent
amounts, no defined quote stage — the workflow you build on top of it will be fragile."

✅ Rewritten:
"If any of these four things is messy, the workflow breaks. A messy discount property.
Inconsistent deal amounts. No clear quote stage. Fix the data first."

❌ Too complex (34 words, buried conditional plus an aside):
"That's a meaningful detail if you've ever dealt with an enterprise buyer's IT team blocking
access to a vendor tool, or a stakeholder who just never gets around to creating an account
for something they'll only use twice."

✅ Rewritten:
"This matters for one reason. Enterprise IT teams often block vendor logins. Buyers skip
creating an account they'll only use twice. A shared link removes both problems."

## No idioms — ever

An idiom that reads fine on the page blocks a non-native reader completely mid-scroll.
On set, "the object earns its keep" killed four consecutive takes — Romeo could not parse
it and finally said "I swear to God, I don't understand what it wants to say."

❌ "With the model in place, the object earns its keep."
✅ "With the model in place, the object starts to bring real value."

If a phrase's meaning is not the literal sum of its words, replace it.

## No insider jargon without an instant explanation

The audience is broader than RevOps insiders. Romeo, after a take: "People don't know what
is the right side of the bow tie. It's very, very niche."

- Default: drop the jargon and say the plain thing ("the part of revenue that comes after
  the signature — renewals and expansion").
- If the concept is genuinely the point of the video, explain it in one plain line the
  first time it appears, and flag it in the Cue Sheet for a supporting graphic.

Terms that triggered this rule on set: "right side of the bow tie," "NRR" unexplained,
segment shorthand. When in doubt, dumb it down — Romeo's words.

## Full sentences, not clipped aphorisms

Punchy staccato fragments read as "robotic" and "professor talking" on the prompter, and
they hide their own meaning. Write the complete natural sentence — it can still be short.

❌ "Today, three things." (Romeo: "It doesn't make sense. It's not natural.")
✅ "In today's video, let's talk about three things."

❌ "What this object actually changes." (Romeo: "Changes *what*? I don't get it.")
✅ "What this object changes inside your HubSpot."

❌ "Your margin melts, and your forecast follows."
✅ "Your margin shrinks, and then your forecast is wrong too."

Compressed noun-phrases must spell out their object. If a line raises "what? / which one? /
of what?" when read cold, expand it.

## Questions: structural yes, audience-directed no

Two different things — only one is banned.

**Banned — audience-directed rhetorical hooks.** Never ask the viewer something and move on:

❌ "Sound familiar?"
❌ "Pretty slick, right?"

**Allowed and encouraged — structural questions the narrator asks and immediately answers.**
This is Romeo's own teaching device in his golden-sample scripts:

✅ "First, what is a tax rate library? It is one list of the taxes your team uses."
✅ "What does a multi-market team mean here? It is any team selling with different prices
across countries or currencies."

**Punctuation carries the intonation.** Any interrogative line must end with a question
mark — a missing "?" makes the reader flatten the line and stop the take. Never write a
question-shaped fragment with a period.

## No hedging — but real conditionals are allowed

Banned as hedges, always: might, perhaps, seems to, tends to, "imagine if," "what if."
Never hedge a claim about how the system behaves. State it directly. If something is
genuinely subject to change (a HubSpot UI label, for example), say so as a fact — "HubSpot
renames this occasionally, check your portal" — not as a hedge.

**"May" and "can" are allowed** when they state a genuine possibility or capability, not a
hedge. Romeo's golden samples use them exactly this way: "The customer may be happy." /
"They can forget, type the wrong number, or discount too much."

**"If" is allowed — and required — for real branching logic.** Tutorials describe systems
with branches, and the golden samples are full of them: "If several books match, the seller
must choose between them." / "If no rule matches, HubSpot assigns no book." What stays
banned is the hypothetical-storytelling conditional that paints an imaginary scene
("imagine if your reps could…", "what if every quote was perfect?"). Test: does the "if"
describe a state the viewer's portal can actually be in? Then keep it. Does it invite the
viewer to daydream? Then cut it.

## No self-deprecating asides

Never script lines that undercut the video's own content. Romeo stopped a take rather than
say one: "I will cut it out when you say 'I know it's boring.' Why are people watching?
Because finance people care."

❌ "I know it's boring, but I will show you."
❌ "This part is dry, bear with me."
✅ Just teach the thing.

## No passive voice

"The workflow is triggered by..." → "The workflow triggers when..."

## Watch triplet overuse

"X, Y, and Z" lists are a known AI-writing tic, and they're also harder to deliver smoothly
out loud than they look on paper. Use at most one triplet per script section; vary with
single points, pairs, or short numbered lists instead. Any list of three or more items
belongs on separate lines with spoken ordinals — see output-export-contract.md §4.2.

## Narrating over demo footage

When the script talks over a screen recording, anchor the reader in it explicitly. The
impersonal register confused Romeo mid-read ("The example names it — what example? This
example is killing me.").

❌ "The example names it referral customers."
✅ "In the example shown here, we have referral customers."

Use "In the example shown here…," "You can see here…," "In this demo portal…" the first
time each new screen or artifact appears.

## Sounds, not just spelling

Read the line aloud before finalizing. Avoid stacking similar consonant sounds across word
boundaries ("six sales stages," "strict script structure") and long strings of unstressed
syllables. If it trips when you say it, rewrite it.

## Numbers

Write numbers the way they're spoken: "twenty percent," not "20%." Exception: on-screen
text callouts can use digits and symbols, since those are read, not spoken.
