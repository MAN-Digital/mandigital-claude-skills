Where the RevOps remit starts, when to hire and what to fix first

The conversation format covers shared definitions, ownership, forecasting, cross-functional handoffs and the first decisions a Revenue Operations leader should stabilise.

How do you define revenue operations to someone who has not had it before?

I’d say it’s the function that owns whether everyone is looking at the same account. Marketing, sales, customer success — they all touch the same customer, and they all keep their own version of the truth about it. RevOps is the team whose job is that there’s only one version.

You know you need it when leadership asks a simple question and gets three different answers depending on who they ask.

At what point should a company hire its first RevOps person?

Earlier than most founders want to hear. Once you have a handful of reps, somebody has to own the instance.

Because if you don’t, then it’s garbage in and it’s garbage out. Nobody’s accountable for what goes in, so nobody trusts what comes out — and that shows up on the forecast call.

“You keep signing new business, but the accounts keep cancelling. It comes in at the top of the funnel and leaks out at the end.”

Carol Chen · VP Revenue Operations · Flywire

What does the first RevOps hire actually own in month one?

Not the dashboard. Definitions first. What is a qualified lead. When does a stage actually move. Who is allowed to change a close date.

If you build reporting on terms nobody agreed on, you’ve just made the disagreement look official.

Where do marketing, sales and customer success most often disagree about the same account?

At the handoffs, and it’s always the same argument — is this account actually ready. Marketing is measuring intent, sales is measuring commitment, and customer success is measuring whether they can keep the promise that got made.

You don’t fix that with a meeting. You fix it with one record all three read from, and the handoff criteria written into it.

What breaks first when a company scales without RevOps?

Agreement breaks first. Two teams pull the same metric and get different numbers, and suddenly the argument is about the spreadsheet instead of the market.

By the time that reaches a board pack, it’s usually been true for two quarters.

How do you build a forecast leadership actually trusts?

By making the inputs inspectable. Trust doesn’t come from being right once — it comes from being able to show why the number moved, and having that hold up when someone pushes on it.

A forecast nobody can interrogate just gets overridden by whoever’s most senior in the room.

What would you tell a CRO inheriting a CRM they did not build?

Don’t rebuild. Find the three decisions the business makes every week, trace what data those actually rest on, and fix that path.

Everything else can wait a quarter. If you spend your first ninety days on a re-architecture, you’ve spent credibility you’re going to want later.

Key takeaways

— RevOps owns whether teams read the same record, not just the reporting.

— The trigger to hire is inconsistent answers to the same question — not headcount.

— Definitions come before dashboards; reporting on undefined terms is noise.

— A forecast earns trust by being inspectable, not by being accurate once.

— Inheriting a CRM: fix the weekly decision path first, not the architecture.
